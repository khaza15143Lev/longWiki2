Overview

High-level synthesis of the knowledge base on premature longitudinal cracking in jointed plain concrete pavements (JPCP).

Current Knowledge

The wiki contains 20 sources spanning 1935–2024, documenting premature longitudinal cracking across diverse climates, subgrades, and construction practices (see longitudinal-cracking-case-studies for a full project inventory). The consensus is that longitudinal cracking is a multifactorial distress involving construction practices, environmental gradients, slab geometry, and traffic loading.

Primary Mechanisms

1. Top-Down Cracking: Multiple studies (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking) confirm that longitudinal cracks initiate at the slab surface, typically 0.6–1.2 m from the longitudinal edge, and propagate downward. This contradicts traditional bottom-up fatigue models.

2. Dowel & Tie Bar Mechanisms: Vertical dowel translation (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking) and subsurface delamination at dowel depth (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking) create localized tensile stresses that initiate cracking pre-traffic. Misaligned dowels (tayabji-trr-1062) and tie bar failures (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs) compromise load transfer and trigger joint separation.

3. Built-In Curling & Nonlinear Gradients: Daytime/summer paving locks in positive built-in curling (quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling). Nonlinear temperature gradients increase top-slab tensile stresses by ~80% compared to linear assumptions (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp).

4. Soil Voiding: Slab curl depressurizes fine-grained soils in dry zones, creating voids beneath the slab and leading to cracking independent of traffic loads (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis).

5. Subbase Erosion: Loss of support from eroded stabilized subbases (HMA/PCC) directly correlates with longitudinal cracking in areas with poor drainage (desantis-roesler-2022-stabilized-support-layers).

Construction Practices

  • Saw-Cut Depth: Shallow saw-cuts at shoulder joints (< D/3 for 12-ft slabs) fail to create a weakened-plane, forcing uncontrolled cracking (ardani-et-al-2003-colorado). A probabilistic crack model confirms saw-cut depth requirements vary by aggregate type: limestone requires 0.39D for 90% confinement, river gravel requires 0.44D (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic).
  • Vibrator Trails: Over-consolidation from malfunctioning paver vibrators reduces air content and creates linear weakened planes (ardani-et-al-2003-colorado).
  • Early-Age Risk: First-night cracking is driven by paving width; widths <24 ft show minimal risk (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking). Late-afternoon paving significantly reduces early-age cracking risk (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements).

Widened Slab Debate

  • SPS-2 / Louisiana: Widened slabs (14–20 ft) with tied shoulders increase longitudinal cracking by 199–374% compared to 12-ft reference slabs (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, saboori-et-al-2021-pavement-me-calibration).
  • Colorado / Wisconsin: Longitudinal cracking in widened slabs is attributed to poor construction quality (saw-cut depth, vibrator trails, drainage) rather than width alone (ardani-et-al-2003-colorado, kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, mateos-harvey-2021-early-age-premature-cracking).
  • Pavement ME: AASHTOWare Pavement ME does not predict longitudinal cracking for JPCP, indicating a gap in mechanistic-empirical design (saboori-et-al-2021-pavement-me-calibration, mateos-harvey-2021-early-age-premature-cracking).

Joint Geometry & Skew

  • Skewed Joints: Skewed transverse joints increase longitudinal cracking potential compared to rectangular joints. Nonlinear gradients exacerbate this effect (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements).
  • Critical Load Position: 4-axle trucks with closely spaced rear axles are the most critical traffic configuration for longitudinal cracking initiation (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking).

Historical Context

The earliest documented case (1935) identifies frost-heave on Colby silt-loam subgrades as the mechanism for longitudinal cracking on Wisconsin Highway 13 (janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13).

Repair & Rehabilitation

  • Slot Stitching: Most cost-effective repair for longitudinal joint separation; outperforms dowel retrofit in long-term performance (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs).
  • ACPA Framework: Trigger/variant framework provides decision trees for diagnosing dowel and tie bar-associated cracking rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking.

Key Findings

  • 14-ft wide slab design is NOT inherently a contributing factor; construction quality is the dominant variable
  • Top-down cracking is the dominant propagation mode; environmental curling and drying shrinkage drive initiation
  • Nonlinear temperature gradients are critical for accurate FEA stress prediction
  • Late-afternoon paving and controlled curing reduce early-age longitudinal cracking risk
  • Stabilized subbases (HMA/PCC) can fail catastrophically via erosion, bypassing traditional drainage design assumptions
  • AASHTOWare Pavement ME cannot predict longitudinal cracking for JPCP; empirical calibration is required

Gaps and Questions

  • Pavement ME lacks a longitudinal cracking prediction model for JPCP
  • Economic analysis of specification changes (e.g., saw-cut depth monitoring) is limited
  • Widened slab debate remains unresolved: is width itself a risk factor, or only a proxy for construction variability?
  • Long-term performance of slot stitching vs. dowel retrofit needs extended monitoring
  • Climate change impacts on drying shrinkage and subbase erosion are not modeled

Longitudinal Cracking in JPCP — Mechanisms, Evidence, and Design Implications

Comprehensive analysis synthesizing 20 sources (1935–2024) on longitudinal cracking in jointed plain concrete pavements (JPCP). This analysis traces the evolution of understanding from the earliest documented case in 1935 through modern 3D FEA and forensic investigations, identifying five distinct cracking mechanisms, the unresolved widened slab debate, and critical gaps in mechanistic-empirical design.

1. Five Distinct Longitudinal Cracking Mechanisms

The literature reveals five mechanisms that initiate longitudinal cracking, each with different triggers, timing, and spatial patterns. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and often interact.

1.1 Top-Down Cracking from Environmental Loading

Most prevalent mechanism. Cracks initiate at the slab surface, typically 0.6–1.2 m (2–4 ft) from the longitudinal edge, and propagate downward. First definitively documented by heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC in Palmdale, California FSHCC test sections, where 37 of 74 slabs cracked under environmental loading before any traffic. Cores confirmed cracking began at the top and propagated downward.

Mechanism: Differential drying shrinkage between the exposed top surface and subbase-restricted bottom creates a moisture gradient. The top contracts more than the bottom, causing slab curling and tensile stresses at the surface. Nighttime negative temperature gradients (cooler top, warmer bottom) amplify these stresses. After 2 months, strains at slab tops increased significantly while bottoms remained constant, confirming differential drying as the primary driver, not thermal contraction.

Slab length dependence: heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC found 5.8 m (19 ft) slabs cracked while 4.0 m (13 ft) slabs remained intact. Maximum stress for longer slabs was ~13% greater than flexural strength (5.14 MPa); shorter slabs experienced 23% lower stress. This explains why longer joint spacings are more vulnerable.

Validation: kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking and yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements confirmed the same top-down pattern in Iowa widened JPCP, with cracks consistently initiating 0.6–1.2 m from the edge. quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling validated the mechanism through LTPP data, showing summer daytime paving locks in positive built-in curling that shifts in-situ temperature profiles, making the surface more susceptible to tensile stress.

1.2 Dowel-Induced Cracking from Vertical Translation

Cracks initiate at transverse joints due to dowel bars installed above mid-depth. First identified by seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking through field observation and 3D FEA (ABAQUS) in Korean expressways.

Mechanism: When a slab curls upward under negative temperature gradient, dowels installed above mid-depth create localized transverse tensile stress concentrations at their location. Properly installed mid-depth dowels do not induce longitudinal cracking. Cracks occur before traffic opening and propagate through cement (not aggregate), confirming early-age environmental loading as the cause.

Field evidence: 6 of 400 joints (1.5%) showed longitudinal cracking; cores confirmed cracks at dowel locations with dowels installed far above mid-depth. Some cores showed horizontal delamination at dowel depth. rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking extended this finding through MIRA scanning and coring at six sites in Wisconsin, Illinois, and Minnesota, finding delamination initiates at dowel depth and migrates upward to the surface, eventually causing spalling. Cracks extend several feet from joints even when no surface distress is visible.

Critical insight from rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking: Early-age microcracking occurs when steel bars (5–10× stiffer than early-age concrete) restrain concrete movement during hydration. Restraint stresses exceed low early-age tensile strength, causing microscopic damage. Microcracks coalesce over time under repeated thermal/moisture/traffic loading into visible delamination. Cores further from joints show cracks passing through aggregate (propagation after concrete gained strength); cores closer to joints show cracks around aggregate (early-age cracking when concrete was weak).

1.3 Soil Voiding from Slab Curl Depressurization

Proposed by xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis through analysis of LTPP SPS-2 data. A distinct mechanism where slab curl depressurizes fine-grained soils in dry zones, creating voids beneath the slab that lead to cracking independent of traffic loading.

Mechanism: When a slab curls upward at edges, it creates negative pressure (suction) in the soil beneath the lifted area. In fine-grained soils and dry climates, this depressurization causes soil consolidation and void formation. The resulting loss of support leads to cracking. Two longitudinal crack patterns identified: (1) single long crack ~1 m from pavement edge adjacent to shoulder; (2) short crack near centerline of slab. Most initiate from slab transverse edges.

Climate dependence: Cracks occur earlier and are more severe in dry climatic zones compared to wet zones. In Arkansas (wet zone), edge pumping occurred as a result of cracking rather than vice versa. The mechanism involves interaction of slab curl, vehicle loading, and foundation soils with low cohesive strengths.

Contrast with other mechanisms: This mechanism is fundamentally different from lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking (thermal/restraint cracking) and heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC (drying shrinkage). It requires coupled hygro-thermo-mechanical analysis rather than purely structural modeling.

1.4 Subbase Erosion and Loss of Support

Identified by desantis-roesler-2022-stabilized-support-layers through field surveys (FWD, MIRA, coring) and laboratory Hamburg Wheel-Tracking Device testing in Illinois. Loss of support from eroded stabilized subbases (HMA/PCC) leads to severe distress including longitudinal cracking that spans multiple slabs.

Mechanism: Water infiltrates through joints and cracks, carrying fine subbase particles out of the support layer. Stabilized subbases can erode catastrophically when drainage is inadequate, creating voids that eliminate slab support. Evidence of erosion found in poor drainage locations (underpasses, sag vertical curves) for pavements with PCC stabilized subbase. CAM II cement content affects erosion resistance — mixes with lower cement contents were more susceptible.

Field evidence: Longitudinal cracking from subbase erosion spans multiple slabs, distinguishing it from isolated top-down cracks. HMA stabilized subbases performed well across tested sections. Bonded concrete overlay over existing composite pavement (HMA over PCC) was erosion-susceptible, contributing to severe failures.

Connection to soil voiding: Both mechanisms involve loss of support, but subbase erosion is driven by water infiltration and particle transport, while soil voiding is driven by slab curl depressurization. xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis noted the two mechanisms can interact in poorly drained areas.

1.5 Construction-Induced Cracking (Saw-Cuts, Vibrator Trails, Tie Bar Failure)

Documented by ardani-et-al-2003-colorado in Colorado Region 1, with construction practices as the root cause. Two primary mechanisms:

Shallow saw-cut depth: Shoulder joint saw-cuts less than D/3 (12-ft slabs) or 0.4D (14-ft slabs) fail to create a weakened-plane, forcing uncontrolled longitudinal cracking to relieve internal stresses. ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043 (Saraf & McCullough) developed a probabilistic crack model confirming that saw-cut depth must account for aggregate type and material variability: limestone aggregate required 0.39D for 90% crack confinement; river gravel required 0.44D.

Vibrator trails: Malfunctioning paver vibrators (set too high or prolonged vibration at low paver speed) over-consolidate concrete, reducing air content and creating linear weakened planes along vibrator paths. Petrographic analysis by CTL showed cores from vibrator trails had consistently lower air content than cores taken 18 inches away in the center of the lane.

Tie bar failure: chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs documented tie bar absence, corrosion, or rupturing at longitudinal construction joints as the root cause of longitudinal joint separation. LTE values below 40% consistently found at separated joints.

2. The Widened Slab Debate

The most contentious issue in the literature is whether widened slabs (14 ft or wider) inherently increase longitudinal cracking risk, or whether observed cracking is attributable to construction quality variability.

2.1 Evidence Supporting Width as Risk Factor

Louisiana study (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking): Survey of 67.5 miles (43 projects, avg age 20.3 years). Widening from 13 ft to 15 ft:

  • Transverse cracking: reduced from 40.9 to 6.7 ft/mi (84% reduction)
  • Longitudinal cracking: increased from 174 to 520 ft/mi (199% increase)

Tied concrete shoulders compared to curb sections:

  • Longitudinal cracking: increased from 174 to 825 ft/mi (374% increase)

FE simulation (ABAQUS, 288 cases): widened slab (12→14 ft) increased longitudinal cracking cases from 25 to 47; tied concrete shoulder produced 55 cases with longitudinal cracking likelihood. Developed first empirical prediction model:

log10(LC) = b0 + b1·TI + b2·L + b3·W + b4·(LW/D²) + b5·Shoulder + b6·Mr + b7·h·∛EBS

R² = 0.5423 (n = 35). Positive coefficients for width (W), shoulder (tied concrete), and traffic (TI).

California calibration (saboori-et-al-2021-pavement-me-calibration): ~4600 lane-miles of PaveM database data from 446 projects. JPCP with widened concrete shoulders (WRF) do not perform well; statistical model predicts performance similar to flexible shoulders. This is because widened shoulders used to be constructed in 14 ft slabs, which are susceptible to longitudinal cracking.

Iowa FEA (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements): Higher top-to-bottom tensile stress ratio and top tensile stress observed with widened slabs (4.3 m/14 ft) with partial-depth tied PCC shoulder compared to regular slabs (3.7 m/12 ft) with full-depth tied PCC shoulder.

2.2 Evidence Attributing Cracking to Construction Quality

Colorado study (ardani-et-al-2003-colorado): The study explicitly cleared 14-ft slab design as a contributing factor, validating CDOT's 1996 adoption based on LTPP SPS-2 research. Cracking attributed to shallow saw-cuts and vibrator trails.

Wisconsin studies (mateos-harvey-2021-early-age-premature-cracking): Colorado found lower cracking in 14-ft panels than 12–13-ft panels. Construction practices identified as the dominant variable.

Mateos & Harvey synthesis (mateos-harvey-2021-early-age-premature-cracking): "Mixed results: SPS-2 and Louisiana studies show increased longitudinal cracking with widened slabs; Colorado and Wisconsin studies attribute cracking to construction issues, not slab width."

2.3 Reconciliation

The evidence suggests both positions are partially correct. Widened slabs increase the distance from the free edge to the slab center, amplifying cumulative frictional restraint stresses (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking). However, this amplification only becomes critical when construction quality is poor (shallow saw-cuts, vibrator trails, inadequate curing, poor dowel alignment). In well-constructed pavements, widened slabs perform adequately; in poorly constructed pavements, the increased slab width amplifies the consequences of construction defects.

Practical implication: Width alone should not be disqualifying, but widened slabs require stricter construction quality control, particularly for saw-cut depth, curing, and dowel placement.

3. Critical Role of Nonlinear Temperature Gradients

Traditional pavement design (AASHTO MEPDG) simplifies temperature distribution to a linear difference between top and bottom surfaces. This simplification significantly underestimates top-slab tensile stresses.

dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp: Nonlinear temperature distribution increases tensile stresses by 80% at the slab top and decreases compressive stresses by 53% at the bottom compared to linear gradient assumption.

quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling: Current AASHTO MEPDG simplifies built-in curling to temperature difference between top and bottom of slab. Actual nonlinear setting temperature profile means concrete cannot reach zero stress state. Positive setting gradient (locked into slab) shifts in-situ temperature profile in negative direction, making it easier for the concrete surface to experience tensile stress.

heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC: Highest concrete tensile stresses developed at the top of the slab under extreme negative temperature gradients (during night). Both shrinkage differential and nonlinear nighttime temperature gradients increase tensile stresses at the top.

Design implication: FEA models using linear temperature gradients significantly underestimate cracking risk. Accurate pavement design requires nonlinear gradient input, especially for skewed joints and widened slabs.

4. Early-Age Cracking Risk and Construction Timing

4.1 First Night After Placement is Critical

lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking established that the critical cracking period is the first night after placement. Nighttime low ambient temperature forces the slab top cooler while the bottom remains hot from hydration heat. Maximum temperature gradient coincides with lowest concrete strength.

Two restraint stress types combine:

  • Axial (frictional) tensile stress: σf = (γc × f × W)/2, increases linearly from free edge
  • Bending (curling) stress from temperature gradient

Total restrained stress = direct summation of both.

4.2 Paving Width Threshold

  • 12-ft wide slabs: stress stays low for all conditions; longitudinal cracking unlikely even with delayed saw-cut
  • 26-ft wide slabs: critically high stress with temperature gradient > -2°F/in; use of wider lanes or delayed longitudinal joint sawing may be unacceptable
  • Paving widths < 24 ft safe for friction coefficient f < 2

Friction coefficient values range from 1.0 (natural subgrade) to 15.0 (lean concrete, cement treated).

4.3 Late-Afternoon Paving Recommendation

yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements observed a site constructed in late afternoon, where concrete set during nighttime and led to less built-in curling, only slightly exhibited longitudinal cracks. quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling quantified that summer daytime paving creates maximum positive built-in curling, increasing both level and frequency of tensile stress on the top of PCC slab.

4.4 Five Risk Factors (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking)

1. High temperature gradient (first night, poor curing) 2. Rough, bonded, or locked slab-base interface 3. Delay in joint sawing in multiple-lane construction 4. High coefficient of thermal expansion (gravel aggregates) 5. Slower rate of strength development

Low-strength concrete at greater risk — cracking potential persists beyond 24–48 hours. High-strength concrete has a safety margin by 24 hours.

5. Joint Geometry Effects

5.1 Skewed Joints

dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp investigated premature cracking in skewed JPCP sections on State Route 49 in Valparaiso, Indiana. Despite 15° skewed joints, ~70% of observed cracks oriented perpendicular to the lane direction. Principal stress directions under combined loading coincide with the longitudinal traffic direction.

yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements confirmed pavements with skewed joints are prone to longitudinal cracks compared to rectangular joints under all loading scenarios. Recommended avoiding skewed joints for widened JPCP with high truck traffic.

5.2 Subgrade Stiffness Reversal

dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp found a counterintuitive result: thermal loading alone produces higher tensile stresses with stiffer subgrade, but combined thermal + wheel loading produces higher stresses with softer subgrade. The increase from thermal to combined loading was 34% for soft clay vs. 21% for stiff clay. This has implications for pavement design in regions with variable subgrade conditions.

6. Traffic Loading Interaction

While environmental loading initiates cracks, traffic loading accelerates propagation and determines service life impact.

Critical load position: kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking identified the four-axle truck with closely spaced rear axles as the most critical configuration. When axle loads are on adjacent slabs, tensile stresses transfer to the critical slab, resulting in very high tensile stress accumulation around the top surface close to the transverse edge.

heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC: Corner loading creates stresses approximately 40% higher than midslab edge loading with positive temperature gradient and no shrinkage gradient. Load plus environmental stress analysis showed the critical failure location at the corner of the slab, not the midslab edge.

Traffic volume correlation: yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements found sites subjected to higher truck traffic volume exhibited a more significant number of longitudinal cracks. However, xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis noted that in dry climates, cracking can occur independent of traffic due to soil voiding.

7. Shoulder Design and Load Transfer

7.1 Tied PCC Shoulders Outperform

kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking: Sites with tied PCC shoulder exhibited fewer longitudinal cracks than sites with HMA shoulders. HMA shoulder not tied to slabs, so no load transfer between slab and HMA shoulder; LTE of only 10% between shoulder and slab bases. Widened or regular slabs with HMA shoulders demonstrate similar behavior to no shoulder.

yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements: Sites with tied PCC shoulders generally outperformed those with HMA and granular shoulders, even though tied PCC sites experienced relatively higher traffic volume. Recommended tied PCC shoulders for widened JPCP with high truck traffic.

7.2 Trade-off: Tied Shoulders Increase Longitudinal Cracking

xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking: Tied concrete shoulders compared to curb sections increased longitudinal cracking from 174 to 825 ft/mi (374% increase). The shoulders change the length/width ratio, creating critical tensile stress at the right wheel path.

Reconciliation: Tied PCC shoulders reduce transverse cracking and improve load transfer, but the increased slab width and altered stress distribution increase longitudinal cracking potential. The net effect depends on construction quality, climate, and traffic.

9. Gaps in Current Design Practice

9.1 AASHTOWare Pavement ME Cannot Predict Longitudinal Cracking

Both saboori-et-al-2021-pavement-me-calibration and mateos-harvey-2021-early-age-premature-cracking confirm that AASHTOWare Pavement ME does not predict longitudinal cracking for JPCP. This is a critical gap, as longitudinal cracking is more prevalent than transverse cracking in many jurisdictions (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking).

9.2 Nonlinear Temperature Gradients Not Modeled

Current design tools assume linear temperature gradients, underestimating top-slab tensile stresses by ~80% (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling).

9.3 Soil Voiding and Subbase Erosion Not Coupled

Existing models treat support as uniform or use Winkler springs with constant k-value. Neither xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis (soil voiding) nor desantis-roesler-2022-stabilized-support-layers (subbase erosion) mechanisms are represented in mechanistic-empirical design.

9.4 Early-Age Construction Risk Not Quantified

lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking and mateos-harvey-2021-early-age-premature-cracking identify construction practices as the dominant variable, but no design tool quantifies the risk of specific construction decisions (paving time, saw-cut depth, curing method) on long-term cracking performance.

Built-In Curling

The permanent or semi-permanent curvature locked into a concrete pavement slab during construction due to temperature and moisture differentials between the top and bottom surfaces.

Mechanism

Built-in curling develops when concrete is placed and cured under non-uniform conditions:

  • Summer/Daytime Paving: Creates positive built-in curling (slab edges curl upward), which increases top-surface tensile stresses under subsequent traffic loading
  • Nonlinear Temperature Gradients: Temperature profiles through the slab thickness are nonlinear, increasing top-slab tensile stresses by ~80% compared to linear gradient assumptions
  • Moisture Differential: Differential drying between the top (exposed) and bottom (subbase contact) surfaces creates permanent moisture-induced curling

Mitigation

  • Late-afternoon paving significantly reduces positive built-in curling risk
  • Controlled curing (fogging, wet burlap) reduces moisture differentials
  • Wider slabs are more susceptible to built-in curling due to longer distance from edge to center

Evidence

  • quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling quantified positive built-in curling from summer paving in JPCP
  • yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements demonstrated late-afternoon paving reduces early-age longitudinal cracking
  • dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp showed nonlinear gradients increase top-slab stresses 80% over linear models

Dowel Bars

Round steel bars placed across transverse joints in jointed concrete pavements to provide load transfer while allowing slab movement (expansion/contraction).

Standard Practice

Per tayabji-trr-1062:

  • Diameter: 1/8 of slab thickness
  • Spacing: 12 inches center-to-center
  • Length: 18 inches
  • Most widely used load transfer device in modern practice

Misalignment Types

Five categories identified by tayabji-trr-1062 (Fig. 1): 1. Horizontal translation — Lateral shift parallel to joint 2. Longitudinal translation — Shift along dowel axis 3. Vertical translation — Up/down shift 4. Vertical skew — Rotation in vertical plane (causes spalling) 5. Horizontal skew — Rotation in horizontal plane (causes spalling + cracking)

Effects of Misalignment

  • Locked joints that restrain slab movement
  • Transverse cracking, corner breaks, spalling around dowels
  • Reduced load transfer effectiveness once spalling occurs
  • Restraint stresses that may contribute to midslab cracking (tayabji-trr-1062, Table 1)

Tolerance Standards

  • Historical FHWA: 1/4 in. per 18-in. length (removed in 1980 Advisory TS140.18)
  • State agencies vary: Illinois (1/8 in. per 12 in.), Georgia (3/8 in. per foot)
  • Laboratory evidence suggests misalignment ≤ 1 in. per 18 in. produces low pullout loads and no spalling at 0.25 in. joint opening (tayabji-trr-1062)

Placement Methods

  • Basket assemblies — Preset dowel cages placed before paving; require rigid basket, proper fastening to subbase
  • Mechanical implantation — Dowels inserted into plastic concrete during paving; higher noncompliance rates observed in field studies

Drying Shrinkage

Volume reduction in concrete caused by moisture loss during curing and service life, generating tensile stresses that can initiate cracking.

Mechanism

Differential drying between the exposed top surface and the subbase-restricted bottom surface creates a moisture gradient. The top surface contracts more than the bottom, causing slab curling and tensile stresses at the surface that can exceed the concrete's early-age tensile strength.

Key Findings

  • heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC found strains at slab tops increased significantly after 2 months while bottoms remained constant, confirming differential drying shrinkage as the primary cause of top-down cracking
  • mateos-harvey-2021-early-age-premature-cracking identified improper curing practices that accelerate surface moisture loss as a major contributor to early-age cracking
  • Longer slabs experience greater cumulative shrinkage stresses, explaining why 5.8m slabs cracked while 4.0m slabs remained intact in FSHCC test sections

Mitigation

  • Controlled curing (wet burlap, fogging, curing compounds) reduces surface moisture loss
  • Shorter joint spacing reduces cumulative shrinkage stress between joints
  • Late-afternoon paving allows concrete to set during cooler nighttime hours, reducing differential drying rates

Expansive Soils

Subgrade soils with high swelling potential that undergo volume change with moisture variation, contributing to differential settlement and longitudinal cracking in concrete pavements.

Characteristics

Per ardani-et-al-2003-colorado (Region 1, Colorado):

  • High plasticity index (PI = 30-39)
  • Lower R-value (resistance to lateral movement)
  • Fraction passing No. 200 sieve close to 100% (highly plastic clayey natural soils) (p. 6)
  • Very sensitive to moisture change; substantial movement with moisture penetration

Distress Pattern

Cracking with differential settlement:

  • Cracks wander between center of lane and wheel paths (Fig. 2, p. 4)
  • Differential settlement visible across cracks
  • Subgrade fill materials may be under-compacted
  • Frost action amplifies the problem when water penetrates through cracks

Mitigation

  • Accurate recognition and prediction of volume change potential before construction (ardani-et-al-2003-colorado, p. 12)
  • Proper treatment of expansive soils prior to construction
  • Adequate subgrade compaction
  • Crack sealing to prevent water infiltration into subgrade

Faulting

Vertical displacement difference between adjacent concrete slabs at a transverse joint or crack. A major contributor to loss of serviceability in jointed concrete pavements.

Causes

Per ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043:

  • Loss of subgrade support (voids beneath slab)
  • Deterioration of load transfer system (dowels, aggregate interlock)
  • Combination of both mechanisms

Impact

  • "Faulting of the slabs is the largest contributor to the loss of serviceability of a PCC pavement" (ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043, p. 1)
  • Creates rough ride, increases dynamic loading on vehicles
  • Accelerates joint deterioration through increased impact stresses

Diagnosis

Nondestructive testing can distinguish between the two causes:

  • Low modulus of subgrade reaction (k < 200 pci) → void/subgrade loss
  • Low modulus of dowel-concrete interaction (G < 200,000 pci) → load transfer failure

Correct diagnosis is critical because rehabilitation methods differ: subsealing for voids vs. load transfer reestablishment for dowel deterioration (ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043, pp. 1-2, p. 7).

Frost Heave

Upward displacement of soil and overlying pavement caused by ice lens formation during freezing of frost-susceptible subgrade soils. A primary mechanism of longitudinal-cracking in cold climates.

Mechanism

Three frost-related scenarios identified by janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13:

Case 1 — Center Heaving (most common): Snow cleared from road center but piled at edges. Center freezes faster, creating upward heave. Tie bars stiffen central slab portion, lifting it as a unit. Longitudinal rupture occurs beyond tie bar ends.

Case 2 — Edge Heaving: Edges freeze and heave before center. Tie bar stiffening causes longitudinal rupture.

Case 3 — Edge Sinking: Non-uniform thaw in spring + heavy vehicles on softened subgrade produces cracking.

Soil Susceptibility

Frost-susceptible soils (per janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13):

  • Colby silt-loam (Wisconsin): fine-grained, dense, plastic; colloidal clay 19-33%; PI 5.5-20.9
  • Discolored water issuing from center joints in spring confirms frost action

Mitigation

  • Elevated "high line" profile with fill from wide ditches reduces frost penetration
  • Mesh reinforcement reduces cracking severity (95% reduction in longitudinal cracking on SH-13)
  • Joint design should permit bending at joint during warping action

Relationship to Expansive Soils

Both frost heave and expansive-soils involve moisture-related volume change, but frost heave is seasonal (freeze-thaw cycle) while expansive soil movement is moisture-driven year-round. ardani-et-al-2003-colorado identified expansive soils (PI=30-39) as cause of differential settlement cracking in Colorado — a different climate mechanism but similar distress pattern.

Finite element analysis program developed at the University of Illinois for modeling jointed concrete pavements under traffic and environmental loading.

ILLI-SLAB models concrete slabs on Winkler or elastic foundation, incorporating joint load transfer, curling and warping stresses, and traffic loading. It was used in the Palmdale FSHCC test sections to analyze top-down cracking mechanisms under combined environmental and traffic loads.

Late-Afternoon Paving

Construction practice of placing concrete during late afternoon hours to allow the slab to set during cooler nighttime conditions, reducing built-in curling risk.

Mechanism

Concrete placed in late afternoon sets during the night, when temperature gradients are less severe and the slab contracts uniformly. This minimizes positive built-in curling (upward edge curl) that occurs when concrete sets under hot daytime conditions with steep surface-to-bottom temperature differentials.

Key Findings

  • yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements observed a site constructed in late afternoon, where concrete set during nighttime and led to less built-in curling, only slightly exhibited longitudinal cracks
  • quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling quantified that summer daytime paving creates maximum positive built-in curling, increasing both level and frequency of tensile stress on top of PCC slab
  • heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC found nighttime negative gradients increase top-surface tensile stresses, but daytime positive gradients during setting are more detrimental for long-term cracking

Design Implications

Recommended practice for JPCP construction, particularly in hot climates and for widened slabs. Should be specified in construction contracts alongside curing requirements.

Load Transfer

The ability of a joint in a concrete pavement to transfer traffic loads from one slab to an adjacent slab. Essential for reducing stresses and deflections under wheel loads.

Mechanisms

Per ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043:

  • Dowel bars — Steel bars across transverse joints providing mechanical load transfer
  • Aggregate interlock — Interlocking of coarse aggregate particles across cracked or jointed faces
  • Keyed joints — Mechanical interlock formed by shaped joint geometry

Quantification

  • Modulus of dowel-concrete interaction (G) — Stiffness parameter characterizing the load transfer system; typical assumed value: 1.5 x 10^6 pci (ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043, p. 5)
  • Joint efficiency — Ratio of deflection on unloaded slab (Point B) to deflection on loaded slab (Point A); measures load transfer effectiveness (ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043, pp. 3-4)
  • Limiting threshold: G < 200,000 pci indicates poor joint system requiring rehabilitation (ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043, p. 7)

Factors Affecting Load Transfer

Parametric study found that dowel diameter, dowel modulus of elasticity, and joint width have minimal effect on corner deflection and joint efficiency. The dominant factor is the modulus of dowel-concrete interaction (ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043, pp. 4-6).

Rehabilitation

Loss of load transfer (low G) requires reestablishment of load transfer through dowel retrofit or joint replacement; subsealing will not restore load transfer (ozbeki-et-al-trr-1043, p. 7).

Longitudinal Cracking

Cracking in portland cement concrete pavements (PCCP) that runs parallel to the direction of traffic, typically along or near the pavement centerline or shoulder joint. One of the most common premature distresses in concrete pavements.

Causes

Two groups identified by ardani-et-al-2003-colorado:

Group 1: Cracking with Differential Settlement

  • High swell potential subgrade soils (high plasticity index, lower R-value)
  • Poor subgrade compaction
  • Manifests as wandering cracks that meander between lane center and wheel paths

Group 2: Cracking without Differential Settlement

  • Shallow saw-cut-depth at shoulder joints (less than D/3 for 12-ft slabs, less than 0.4D for 14-ft slabs)
  • vibrator-trails from malfunctioning or improperly adjusted paver vibrators
  • Cracks appear straight and at consistent distance from pavement edge

Evidence

  • Cores from non-working shoulder joints showed saw-cuts 35-50% shallower than specification (ardani-et-al-2003-colorado, p. 10)

Temperature profiles through concrete slab thickness that deviate from linear assumptions, creating higher tensile stresses at the slab surface.

Mechanism

Probabilistic Crack Model

A statistical model developed by saraf and mccullough to predict the probability that longitudinal cracking will be confined within a saw-cut groove rather than forming away from it.

Model Formulation

Per saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic:

  • Cracking occurs along saw-cut section (x-x) when tensile strength ratio R = T1/T2 ≤ 1.0
  • T1, T2 = tensile strengths at saw-cut vs. adjacent section
  • R modeled as ratio of lognormally distributed variables: concrete tensile strength (t1, t2) and pavement thickness (D1, D2)
  • ln R is normally distributed with mean RL and standard deviation σRL derived from component variances (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 9–10, Eqs. 8–12)

Key Parameters

  • Depth ratio (D1/D2) — Ratio of remaining thickness at saw-cut to full slab thickness; lower ratio = deeper cut = higher probability of crack confinement
  • Standard deviation of tensile strength — Measures construction quality variability; lower SD = more uniform concrete = better crack control
  • Aggregate type — Affects tensile strength variability; limestone produced lower SD than river gravel

Field Verification

  • Project 1 (river gravel, TX-288): predicted 67%, observed 69%
  • Project 2 (limestone, TX-288): predicted 91%, observed 99%
  • For 90% crack confinement: river gravel needs 0.44D cut depth; limestone needs 0.39D (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 10–12, Fig. 3)

Practical Implications

Reducing construction variability (quartering standard deviations) can achieve same crack confinement with shallower saw-cuts, saving construction costs (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 12–13).

Slab Geometry

The length, width, and thickness dimensions of a concrete pavement slab, which determine the distribution of critical tensile stresses under traffic loading and thus influence whether cracking occurs in the transverse or longitudinal direction.

Longitudinal Cracking Ratio (LCR)

Defined by xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking:

  • LCR = σCx / σCy (transverse critical tensile stress / longitudinal critical tensile stress)
  • LCR < 0.8: slab unlikely to crack longitudinally
  • LCR 0.8-1.0: cracks may initiate in either direction
  • LCR 1.0-1.2: longitudinal cracks very likely
  • LCR > 1.2: slab will definitely crack longitudinally

Geometry Effects

Per xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking:

  • Wider slabs reduce length/width ratio, shifting critical stress location from longitudinal edge to transverse joint
  • Tied concrete shoulders further change geometry, creating critical tensile stress at right wheel path
  • Thinner slabs develop more longitudinal cracking than thicker slabs
  • Longer slabs have larger potential for transverse rather than longitudinal cracking
  • Width-to-thickness ratio of 1.2-1.5 recommended to minimize cracking (Wisconsin study)

Design Implications

  • AASHTO Pavement ME considers mid-slab transverse cracking as primary JPCP distress; longitudinal cracking NOT predicted
  • Widened slabs and tied shoulders effectively reduce transverse cracking but increase longitudinal cracking — a design tradeoff requiring mechanistic-empirical model
  • NCHRP 1-51 investigating slab/underlying layer interaction for MEPDG

Temperature difference between the top and bottom surfaces of a concrete pavement slab, driving curling and warping stresses.

Mechanism

Positive gradients (top warmer than bottom) cause downward curling; negative gradients (top cooler) cause upward curling. Nighttime negative gradients are particularly severe, creating high top-surface tensile stresses.

Key Findings

  • heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC found highest concrete tensile stresses develop at top of slab under extreme negative temperature gradients (during night)
  • quan-et-al-2014-built-in-curling demonstrated nonlinear temperature profiles through slab thickness increase top-slab tensile stresses by ~80% compared to linear assumptions
  • dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp incorporated nonlinear gradients in 3D FEA, showing critical role in skewed joint cracking

A concrete shoulder structurally connected to the main pavement slab via tie bars, providing continuous load transfer and support at the longitudinal edge.

Tied concrete shoulders act as an extension of the main slab, distributing traffic loads across a wider area and reducing edge stresses. Tie bars prevent separation at the longitudinal construction joint between the main slab and shoulder.

Top-Down Cracking

A failure mechanism in concrete pavements where cracks initiate at the slab surface and propagate downward, contrary to traditional bottom-up fatigue models.

Mechanism

Top-down longitudinal cracking initiates at the surface, typically 0.6–1.2 m from the longitudinal edge. The mechanism is driven by:

  • Environmental curling: Differential temperature and moisture gradients cause the slab to curl upward at edges, creating tensile stresses at the top surface
  • Drying shrinkage: Early-age moisture loss creates tensile stresses at the slab surface that exceed concrete tensile strength
  • Traffic interaction: Once initiated, traffic loading accelerates downward propagation, though initiation is environmental

Evidence

  • heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC first documented top-down cracking as the dominant longitudinal cracking mode in JPCP
  • kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking confirmed surface initiation 0.6–1.2 m from the edge in Iowa widened slabs
  • xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis validated the mechanism through LTPP data and fracture mechanics modeling

Vibrator Trails

Linear weakened planes in concrete pavement caused by malfunctioning or improperly adjusted paver vibrators. Appear as straight longitudinal cracks at a consistent distance from the pavement edge.

Mechanism

  • Vibrator frequency set too high OR prolonged vibration at low paver speed
  • Over-consolidates concrete mix
  • Causes non-uniform dispersion of aggregate particles
  • Reduces air content in the vibrator path
  • Creates a weakened plane where longitudinal cracking initiates

Evidence

ardani-et-al-2003-colorado documented:

  • Cores from vibrator trails showed consistently lower air content than cores taken 18 inches away in lane center (pp. 7-8)
  • Cracks along vibrator trails appeared straight and at consistent distance from pavement edge (Fig. 1, p. 4; Fig. 5, p. 7)
  • Occurred even in areas where shoulder joint saw-cuts were at proper depth (p. 8)
  • Split tensile strength testing inconclusive for strength difference (Table 1, p. 9)

Prevention

CDOT specification requires paving contractors to equip machines with vibrator frequency monitoring devices (ardani-et-al-2003-colorado, p. v, p. 12), following Iowa DOT's specification approach.

Weakened Plane

A deliberately created zone of reduced cross-section in a concrete pavement slab that controls where cracking occurs. Saw-cut joints are the primary method of creating weakened planes in PCCP.

Design Principle

Concrete shrinks and experiences thermal stresses during curing and service. A weakened plane ensures that when internal stresses exceed concrete tensile strength, cracking occurs at the joint rather than randomly within the slab.

Key Requirements

Per ardani-et-al-2003-colorado:

  • Saw-cut must be deep enough to create effective weakened plane: D/3 for 12-ft slabs, 0.4D for 14-ft slabs
  • Shallow cuts fail to create weakened plane, causing uncontrolled longitudinal cracking to relieve stresses (p. v)
  • Cores from "non-working" shoulder joints (where joint did not crack through) confirmed saw-cut deficiency (Fig. 9, p. 10)

Failure Modes

When weakened plane fails to form:

  • Cracks develop elsewhere in the slab to relieve internal stresses
  • Cracking may follow vibrator trails (secondary weakened plane from over-consolidation)
  • Random longitudinal cracking replaces controlled joint cracking

Widened Slab

Concrete pavement slabs wider than the standard 12-ft lane width, typically 13-15 ft, often used with tied concrete shoulders to improve structural capacity and reduce transverse cracking.

Tradeoff

Per xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking:

  • Benefit: Widening 13→15 ft reduced transverse cracking 84% (40.9→6.7 ft/mi)
  • Cost: Longitudinal cracking increased 199% (174→520 ft/mi)
  • Mechanism: Changed length/width ratio shifts critical fatigue damage from longitudinal edge to transverse joint, creating longitudinal crack potential

Historical Context

  • ardani-et-al-2003-colorado found 14-ft slab design NOT a contributing factor to premature construction-related longitudinal cracking in Colorado (validated LTPP SPS-2)
  • xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking found widened slabs DO increase longitudinal cracking under traffic fatigue in Louisiana
  • Resolution: Colorado studied early-age construction defects (saw-cut depth, vibrators); Louisiana studied long-term fatigue. Both can be true in different contexts.

Design Guidance

  • xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking recommends width-to-thickness ratio of 1.2-1.5 (Wisconsin study reference)
  • Slab geometry (length, width, thickness) should be carefully designed to minimize longitudinal cracking potential
  • AASHTO Pavement ME does NOT currently predict longitudinal cracking for JPCP

Report No. CDOT-DTD-R-2003-1 Published January 2003 Sponsor Colorado Department of Transportation (CDOT), FHWA

Overview

This Colorado Department of Transportation report documents the investigation of premature longitudinal cracking in portland cement concrete pavements (PCCP) across three sites in Region 1: I-70 near Agate, US-287 at Kit Carson, and I-70 at Deer Trail. The study identifies two primary causes and results in two new CDOT specifications aimed at preventing recurrence.

Key Claims

1. Shallow saw-cut depth is the primary cause — Shoulder joint saw-cuts less than 1/3 of slab thickness (D/3 for 12-ft slabs, 0.4D for 14-ft slabs) fail to create a weakened plane, causing random longitudinal cracking to relieve internal stresses (p. v, p. 11).

2. Malfunctioning vibrators create vibrator trails — Vibrators set too high or prolonged vibration at low paver speed over-consolidates concrete, reducing air content and forming weakened planes along vibrator paths (p. v, pp. 7-8).

3. 14-ft slab design is not a contributing factor — The study explicitly cleared the wider slab design, validating CDOT's 1996 adoption based on LTPP SPS-2 research (p. v, p. 11).

4. Expansive subgrade soils contribute to differential settlement — Soils with high plasticity index (PI=30-39) and poor compaction cause longitudinal cracking with differential settlement, though these were isolated cases in the study (p. 4, p. 6, p. 11).

5. Core evidence confirms air content reduction — Petrographic analysis by CTL showed cores from vibrator trails had consistently lower air content than cores taken 18 inches away in the center of the lane (p. 7, Appendix C).

  • "Examination of cores from several locations on US-287 and I-70 revealed longitudinal joint saw-cuts that were less than 1/3 of the thickness of pavement, i.e., not deep enough to instigate weakened plane cracking. As a result, longitudinal cracks developed to relieve the internal stresses in the concrete." (p. v)
  • "Vibrator frequency set too high and/or prolonged vibration at low paver speed was determined to be the main factor that caused longitudinal cracking along the vibrator trails." (p. v)
  • "This study found that the 14-foot-wide slab design was not among the causes of longitudinal cracking. CDOT adopted the 14-foot slab design in 1996 based on the results of the LTPP SPS-2 experiment, and a supplemental study conducted by Dr. Michael Darter of ERES." (p. 11)

Repairs of Longitudinal Joint Separations and Their Performances

Journal Construction and Building Materials, Vol. 54, pp. 496–503 Sponsor Texas Department of Transportation (TxDOT)

Overview

Evaluates three repair methods for longitudinal joint separations and slab faulting in CRCP and JCP across seven Texas field projects: slot stitching, stapling, and cross stitching. Provides laboratory comparison, field case studies, cost analysis, and performance recommendations.

Key Claims

1. Tie bar failure is root cause — Absence, corrosion, or rupturing of tie bars at longitudinal construction joints causes larger slab deflections, pumping, and ultimately lane separation, cracking, and faulting (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs, pp. 496-497).

2. Low LTE indicates separation risk — Load Transfer Efficiency (LTE) values below 40% consistently found at longitudinal joints with separation; determined from FWD testing (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs, p. 497).

3. Slot stitching most cost-effective — Restores load transfer and horizontal anchorage using deformed bars installed in saw-cut slots at mid-depth. Ultimate shear load: 12.0 kips vs. 6.7 kips for stapling. IH35 project: $160/bar (741 bars), half the cost of full-depth repair (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs, pp. 498-499).

4. Stapling performance depends on filler material — U-shaped bars at max 3-in. depth (vs. 6-in. for slot stitching); filled with low-modulus elastomeric concrete. Houston district: some projects performing well >8 years. Material B (elastomeric) outperformed Material C (cracking, spalling, debonding observed). Stapling costs 2x more than slot stitching (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs, pp. 500-501).

5. Cross stitching limited to tight cracks — Alternating diagonal holes with #6 deformed bars and epoxy. Failed on US59 (faulted slabs, poor LTE) within 2 years. Effective on SH289 (tight cracks, strong base support, good aggregate interlock), but effectiveness uncertain — unstitched cracks showed similar performance (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs, pp. 501-502).

6. Weak base/subgrade causes recurring FDR failures — US75: DCP showed base modulus 15-30 ksi vs. expected >50 ksi; subgrade CBR = 5, modulus = 7 ksi. Full-depth repairs failed in <1 year because weak foundation not replaced (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs, p. 499).

7. Class P concrete filler recommended — Least expansive slot filler material; performed well without longitudinal movement after repairs on IH35 and US75. Requirements: 7-day flexural ≥570 psi or compressive ≥3500 psi; 28-day flexural ≥680 psi or compressive ≥4400 psi (chen-2014-longitudinal-joint-repairs, pp. 498-499).

Journal Engineering Failure Analysis 13, pp. 898–913 Sponsor Joint Transportation Research Program, Indiana Department of Transportation (SPR-2643)

Overview

Investigates premature transverse cracking in skewed JPCP sections on State Route 49 in Valparaiso, Indiana, using 3D finite element analysis (ANSYS). Demonstrates that combined thermal and wheel loading with nonlinear temperature gradients predicts both crack location and ~4-year design life matching observed field performance.

Key Claims

1. Nonlinear gradients critical — Nonlinear temperature distribution increases tensile stresses by 80% at slab top and decreases compressive stresses by 53% at bottom compared to linear gradient assumption (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, pp. 906).

2. Cracks perpendicular to traffic, not joints — Despite 15° skewed joints, ~70% of observed cracks oriented perpendicular to lane direction. Principal stress directions under combined loading coincide with longitudinal traffic direction (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, pp. 899, 912).

3. Subgrade stiffness effect reverses under combined loading — Thermal loading alone: stiffer subgrade → higher tensile stresses. Combined thermal + wheel: softer subgrade → higher tensile stresses. Increase from thermal to combined loading was 34% for soft clay vs. 21% for stiff clay (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, pp. 907-908).

4. Shrinkage strains amplify stresses — Initial shrinkage simulated as equivalent -5°C top surface temperature change increased maximum tensile stress by 20% (thermal only) and 23% (combined loading) (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, pp. 910-911).

5. Fatigue prediction matches field performance — PCA fatigue equation: SR = 3400/4482 = 0.76 → N = 376 cycles. Assuming severe conditions occur once per season, design life ≈ 4 years, matching observed 3-4 year premature failure (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, pp. 911-912).

6. Longer slabs more susceptible — 5.49m (18ft) and 5.79m (19ft) slabs more likely to crack than 3.66m (12ft) and 3.96m (13ft) slabs (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, p. 900).

7. Joint opening and rotation — Negative temperature gradients cause upward curling and joint opening. Maximum differential rotation at skewed joint: 0.048° without traffic, 0.014° with traffic loads (dere-asgari-sotelino-archer-2006-skewed-jpcp, pp. 909-910).

Journal Transportation Research Record 1712, Paper No. 00-0974 Sponsor California Department of Transportation (Caltrans), UC Berkeley Pavement Research Center

Overview

Investigates top-down cracking of FSHCC test sections in Palmdale, California under environmental loading (no traffic). Uses ILLI-SLAB (ILSL2) FEA and field instrumentation to demonstrate that differential drying shrinkage combined with nonlinear negative temperature gradients causes top-down cracking, with longer slabs cracking while shorter slabs remain intact.

Key Claims

1. Top-down cracking from environmental loading — 37 of 74 slabs cracked under environmental influences before any traffic loading. Cores confirmed cracking began at top of slabs and propagated downward (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, pp. 13-14, 421-423).

2. Differential drying shrinkage primary cause — After 2 months, strains at tops of slabs increased significantly while bottoms remained constant. Increase due to drying shrinkage, not thermal contraction. Differential strains through slab thickness resulted in slab bending stresses exceeding concrete strength (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, pp. 427-434).

3. Longer slabs crack, shorter don't — Maximum stress for longer (5.8m) slabs was ~13% greater than flexural strength (5.14 MPa). Maximum stress in shorter (4m) slabs was 23% lower than longer slabs and less than flexural strength. Explains why longer slabs cracked, shorter remained largely uncracked (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, pp. 400-406).

4. Nighttime worst-case gradients — Highest concrete tensile stresses developed at top of slab under extreme negative temperature gradients (during night). Both shrinkage differential and nonlinear nighttime temperature gradients increase tensile stresses at top (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, pp. 386-392).

5. Corner loading critical — Load plus environmental stress analysis showed critical failure location at corner of slab, not midslab edge. Stresses for midslab edge loading with positive temperature gradient and no shrinkage gradient were ~40% lower than corner loading case (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, pp. 407-417, 448-451).

6. Permanent upward curl — JDMDs showed significant corner curling with as much as 2.5mm daily movement. Corner deflection data suggested slab corners permanently curled upward due to differential drying shrinkage, resulting in unsupported corner condition at all times (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, pp. 435-443).

7. FSHCC not inherently high-shrinkage — Laboratory testing showed some CsA cements could have lower shrinkage than Type II cements. FSHCC does not necessarily indicate high shrinkage differentials (heath-roesler-2000-top-down-cracking-FSHCC, pp. 455-468).

Publication Highway Research Board Proceedings, pp. 157-169 Sponsor University of Wisconsin, Wisconsin Highway Commission

Overview

Classic 1935 field study documenting excessive longitudinal cracking in concrete pavements built on Colby silt-loam soil in north-central Wisconsin. Establishes frost heave as the primary mechanism and evaluates the effects of tie bar spacing, reinforcement, and roadbed elevation on crack development.

Key Claims

1. Cracking confined to specific soil type — Excessive longitudinal cracking found exclusively on Colby silt-loam soil; cracking ceased abruptly when road passed to other soil types. Soil: exceedingly fine-grained, dense, plastic, subject to excessive frost heaving (janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13, pp. 157-159).

2. Frost heave is the mechanism — Three frost-related scenarios identified:

  • Case 1 (center heaving): Snow removed from center, edges insulated; faster freezing at center causes upward heave. Stiffened central portion (from tie bars) lifts as unit, causing rupture beyond tie bar ends.
  • Case 2 (edge heaving): Edges heave before center; tie bar stiffening causes longitudinal rupture.
  • Case 3 (edge sinking): Non-uniform thaw + heavy vehicles on soft subgrade produce cracking (janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13, pp. 166-167, Fig. 6).

3. Tie bars stiffen central portion — Tie bars lacing two slabs together prevent lateral movement but also stiffen the center. Cracks form 2.5-4 ft from centerline, approximately at ends of tie bars. Closer tie bar spacing (2 ft) produced MORE cracking than wider spacing (4 ft) (janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13, pp. 166-168).

4. Reforcement dramatically reduces cracking:

  • 49-lb mesh: longitudinal cracking reduced from 88 ft/100 ft to 4.2 ft/100 ft (95% reduction); transverse cracking reduced 44%
  • 1/2-in. bar mat: longitudinal cracking reduced from 40.5 ft/100 ft to 5.2 ft/100 ft (87% reduction); transverse cracking reduced 27%
  • All cracks in reinforced sections were extremely narrow/tight (janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13, Tables II-IV, VI).

5. "High line" profile reduces cracking — Elevated roadbed with fill material from wide ditches at either side reduced frost heave effects. SH-29 east of Abbotsford (high profile) showed little cracking (janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13, pp. 167-168).

6. Pavement cross-sections show center peak — Accurate surveys confirmed pavement heaved upward at centerline in frost-affected areas, consistent with Case 1 theory (janda-1935-wisconsin-highway-13, Fig. 7).

Notable Quotes

  • "The Colby silt-loam soil is an exceedingly fine-grained soil, dense and plastic... conducive to frost heaving, and this action is probably the real reason for the longitudinal cracking of the concrete pavements in this area." (p. 166)
  • "The longitudinal joint should be so designed as to maintain the central edges of the slab at the same elevation yet permit bending at the joint when a warping action is present." (p. 169)

Overview

Numerical analysis of longitudinal cracking mechanism in widened JPCP using FEA. Identifies critical four-axle truck load configuration with axle loads near transverse edges as highest cracking scenario. Compares shoulder design alternatives for mitigation.

Key Claims

1. All longitudinal cracks are top-down — Field investigations in Iowa confirmed all longitudinal cracks are top-down cracks starting mostly from transverse joints about 0.6-1.2m (2-4ft) away from widened slab edges (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 89-92).

2. Critical loading scenario — Four-axle truck with center of axle loads placed close to transverse edges identified as critical loading scenario. When axle loads on adjacent slabs, tensile stresses transferred to critical slab, resulting in very high tensile stress accumulation around top surface close to transverse edge (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 845-859).

3. Negative temperature gradient amplifies — High negative temperature gradient (slabs curl up) combined with axle loads on adjacent slabs transfers top tensile stresses to critical slabs, creating extremely high top tensile stresses around transverse joints (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 851-859).

4. Tied PCC shoulders outperform HMA — Sites with tied PCC shoulder exhibited fewer longitudinal cracks than sites with HMA shoulders. Tied PCC shoulder design performs better than HMA and granular shoulders for longitudinal crack potential (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 91-92, 912-914).

5. Widened slabs increase cracking potential — Higher top-to-bottom tensile stress ratio and top tensile stress observed with widened slabs (4.3m/14ft) with partial-depth tied PCC shoulder compared to regular slabs (3.7m/12ft) with full-depth tied PCC shoulder (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 866-873).

6. HMA shoulders minimal effect — HMA shoulder not tied to slabs, so no load transfer between slab and HMA shoulder; LTE of only 10% between shoulder and slab bases. Widened or regular slabs with HMA shoulders demonstrate similar behavior to no shoulder (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 883-892).

7. Five mitigation recommendations:

  • Longitudinal cracks mainly in traffic lane 0.3-0.6m (2-4ft) from slab edge
  • Shorter joint spacing reduces curling/warping and longitudinal cracking
  • Most cracks start from slab transverse joints
  • Proper dowel bar installation helps mitigate (restrains vertical deflection)
  • Tied PCC shoulder design outperforms HMA and granular shoulders (kaya-et-al-2019-widened-jpcp-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 896-914)

Overview

Presents an analytical approach using JSLAB finite element analysis and maturity method to predict early-age longitudinal cracking potential in JPCP. Develops graphical stress charts for 3,072 construction condition combinations to compare restrained stresses against early-age concrete strength development.

Key Claims

1. Critical cracking period is first night after placement — Nighttime low ambient temperature forces slab top cooler while bottom remains hot from hydration heat. Maximum temperature gradient coincides with lowest concrete strength (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 1323-1324).

2. Two restraint stress types combine — Axial (frictional) tensile stress increases linearly from free edge; bending (curling) stress from temperature gradient. Total restrained stress = direct summation of both (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 1327-1328).

3. Paving width is critical factor:

  • 12-ft wide slabs: stress stays low for all conditions; longitudinal cracking unlikely even with delayed saw-cut
  • 26-ft wide slabs: critically high stress with temperature gradient > -2°F/in; use of wider lanes or delayed longitudinal joint sawing may be unacceptable (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 1330-1331).

4. Friction coefficient matters — σf = (γc × f × W)/2. Values range from 1.0 (natural subgrade) to 15.0 (lean concrete, cement treated). Critical stress conditions do not develop for f < 2. Paving widths < 24 ft safe for f < 2 (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 1328, 1337).

5. Maturity method for strength prediction — Time-Temperature Factor (TTF): M(t) = Σ(Ta-T0)Δt. High-strength concrete at 24 hrs: 420 psi flexural, E = 2.3×10⁶ psi. Low-strength concrete at 24 hrs: 180 psi flexural, E = 1.3×10⁶ psi (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 1334-1335).

6. Five risk factors identified:

  • High temperature gradient (first night, poor curing)
  • Rough, bonded, or locked slab-base interface
  • Delay in joint sawing in multiple-lane construction
  • High coefficient of thermal expansion (gravel aggregates)
  • Slower rate of strength development (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking, p. 1340)

7. Low-strength concrete at greater risk — Cracking potential persists beyond 24-48 hours; temperature gradient > 3.0°F/in creates critical condition at later ages. High-strength concrete has safety margin by 24 hours (lim-tayabji-2005-early-age-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 1336-1337).

Notable Quotes

  • "For routine pavement construction with well prepared and protected base surfaces and normal ambient temperature conditions, the potential for early-age longitudinal cracking is not considerable, even if for full width (e.g., 26 ft) paving. However, if the exceptional temperature condition is expected, it may be prudent to delay paving, considering night-time paving or partial width (lane at a time) paving." (p. 1340)

  • Mixture design: cement type/content, aggregate type, SCMs, admixtures
  • Pavement design: joint spacing, sawcut depth, bond breaker, old-new concrete connection

Overview

Comprehensive Phase 1 study evaluating cracking related to dowel bars and tie bars in JPCP through literature review and field investigations at six sites in Wisconsin, Illinois, and Minnesota. Identifies delamination cracking at dowel bar depth as primary mechanism, with cracks initiating during early age and propagating under combined environmental and traffic loading.

Key Claims

1. Delamination initiates at dowel depth — Cracks start around dowel bar depth near transverse joints as horizontal delamination cracks, then migrate upward to surface, eventually causing spalling. MIRA scans and coring confirm cracks extend several feet from joints even when no surface distress visible (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 504-505, 69-85).

2. Early-age microcracking mechanism — Steel bars (5-10× stiffer than early-age concrete) restrain concrete movement during hydration. Restraint stresses exceed low early-age tensile strength, causing microscopic damage. Microcracks coalesce over time under repeated thermal/moisture/traffic loading into visible delamination (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 480-481, 540-541).

3. Cracks propagate through aggregate — Cores further from joints show cracks passing through aggregate (indicating propagation after concrete gained strength). Cores closer to joints show cracks around aggregate (indicating early-age cracking when concrete was weak) (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 505, 85).

4. Delamination visible on apparently intact joints — MIRA scanning revealed subsurface delamination on joints with no visible surface distress. Cracks extend through multiple dowel bars, sometimes full transverse joint length (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 60-69).

5. Spalls progress rapidly — Time history from Illinois Tollway shows spalls progress from small to large within 2 years. Spalling equally likely on approach or leave side of joint (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 57-59).

6. Six cracking mechanisms identified:

  • Restraint cracking (temperature drop, hot-weather paving, high evaporation)
  • Subsidence/settlement cracking (high slump, low cover, large bar size)
  • Plastic shrinkage cracking
  • Misalignment-induced cracking (locked joints)
  • Chloride-induced delamination/corrosion
  • Tie bar over-reinforcing and edge restraint from tied shoulders (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 6-21)

7. ACPA trigger/variant framework — Early restraint cracking requires triggers (≥25°F temperature drop, >90°F paving, >0.1 lb/ft² evaporation) combined with variants (high friction base, late sawing, high cement factor, shrinkage-susceptible mix). Two triggers + three variants = "definite" cracking risk (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 7-9).

8. Field sites show common pattern — Six sites (WI: I-39, US 53; IL: I-94; MN: TH 610) all exhibited large spalls (≥1 ft) at transverse joints within 10-15 years. Spalls distinguishable from durability-related spalling by size, depth, and lack of hairline cracking (rao-abdualla-sufian-2024-dowel-tie-bar-cracking, pp. 45-55).

Controlling Longitudinal Cracking in Concrete Pavements

Publication Transportation Research Record 1043, pp. 8–13 Sponsor Pennsylvania Department of Transportation, FHWA Institution University of Texas at Austin

Overview

Develops a probabilistic model to estimate the depth of saw cut needed to control longitudinal cracking within the groove in wide concrete pavements. Uses lognormal distributions of concrete tensile strength and pavement thickness to predict crack confinement probability. Validates the model against field observations from two Texas projects near Houston.

Key Claims

1. Probabilistic crack confinement model — Cracking occurs along the saw-cut (x-x) when the tensile strength ratio R = T1/T2 ≤ 1.0, where T1 is tensile strength at the saw-cut and T2 is tensile strength away from it. The model uses lognormal distributions of concrete tensile strength and pavement thickness to estimate the probability P[R ≤ 1.0] that cracks will be confined within the groove (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 8–10).

2. Aggregate type determines required saw-cut depth — Two projects near Houston, Texas:

  • River gravel mix: required 0.44D saw-cut depth for 90% crack confinement
  • Limestone aggregate mix: required only 0.39D for 90% confinement

    • The limestone mix had lower standard deviation in tensile strength, enabling better crack control at shallower cuts (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 10–12, Fig. 3).

3. Field verification matches predictions:

  • Project 1 (river gravel, TX-288 inside I-610): predicted 67% vs. observed 69% cracking along saw-cut joint
  • Project 2 (limestone, TX-288 outside I-610): predicted 91% vs. observed 99%

    • Both showed reasonable match between model and field observations (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 10–11, Table 1).

4. 31% of river gravel cracks escaped the groove — On Project 1, 31% of cracks developed away from the saw cut, compared to only 1% on the limestone project. This highlights the role of material variability in crack control (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 10).

5. Reducing variability allows shallower cuts — Sensitivity analysis showed that halving or quartering standard deviations of concrete strength substantially increases crack confinement at the same depth ratio, potentially saving construction costs. Uniformity of concrete mix strength (represented by standard deviation) is a critical construction quality variable (saraf-mccullough-1986-sawcut-depth-probabilistic, pp. 12–13).

Notable Quotes

  • "A lower value of standard deviation obtained for concrete mix using lime rock aggregate in the mix was responsible for confining more cracks within the saw cut compared with the mix using river gravel aggregate." (p. 8)
  • "A sensitivity analysis of the model indicated that substantial reduction in saw-cut depth can be achieved if the variability of concrete strength during construction can be reduced." (p. 8)

Journal KSCE Journal of Civil Engineering 17(2), pp. 395-402 Sponsor National Research Foundation of Korea (NRF 2010-0007816), Korea Expressway Corporation

Overview

Investigates longitudinal cracking at transverse joints in JCP through field observation and 3D FEA (ABAQUS). Identifies vertical translation of dowel bars from mid-depth as primary cause, with stress concentrations occurring when slabs curl under environmental loading before traffic opening.

Key Claims

1. Vertical dowel translation is primary cause — Dowels installed above mid-depth cause localized transverse tensile stress concentrations at their location when slab curls up. Properly installed mid-depth dowels do not induce longitudinal cracking (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 395-396, 401).

2. Cracks occur before traffic opening — Field cracks observed before pavement opened to traffic, propagated through cement (not aggregates), indicating early-age environmental loading as primary cause (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 396).

3. Stress concentration localized — Transverse stress increment at vertically translated dowel is localized; does not affect whole stress distribution. Multiple translated dowels do not interact — each produces same stress increment independently (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 398-399).

4. Center slabs more vulnerable — Stress increment at translated dowel location increases as dowel moves from slab edge toward center. Translated dowels near slab center produce stress larger than maximum center stress, creating high cracking probability (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 397-398).

5. Five factors increase cracking risk — Transverse stresses increase with: (1) higher concrete elastic modulus, (2) higher thermal expansion coefficient, (3) higher foundation stiffness, (4) larger vertical temperature gradient, (5) greater vertical translation amount from mid-depth (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 399-400).

6. Bond characteristic affects crack initiation — When translated dowel is free to move: stress concentrated on slab surface and under dowel hole. When perfectly bonded: large stresses both on surface and above dowel hole. Crack initiation position depends on bond condition (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 400-401).

7. Field validation — 6 of 400 joints (1.5%) showed longitudinal cracking. Cores confirmed cracks at dowel locations with dowels installed far above mid-depth. Some cores showed horizontal delamination at dowel depth (seo-kim-2013-dowel-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 396).

Notable Quotes

  • "To mitigate longitudinal cracking at the joint, dowel bars should be installed at the mid-depth of the slab and cares must be taken when installing dowel bars to prevent their vertical translation." (p. 401)

Overview

Comprehensive investigation into allowable levels of dowel bar misalignment at concrete pavement joints. Combines theoretical analysis attempts, laboratory pullout testing on slab specimens, and review of field studies from multiple state DOTs to establish placement tolerance criteria.

Key Claims

1. Five types of dowel misalignment — Horizontal translation, longitudinal translation, vertical translation, vertical skew, and horizontal skew. Only vertical skew and horizontal skew cause spalling; horizontal/vertical skew also causes cracking and reduces load transfer (tayabji-trr-1062, Fig. 1).

2. Pullout loads low for misalignment < 1 in. — Laboratory tests on 8-in. and 10-in. slabs showed pullout loads remain relatively low for misalignment ≤ 1 in. per 18-in. dowel length at joint opening of 0.25 in. No spalling observed below this threshold (tayabji-trr-1062, pp. 52-54, Tables 7-8).

3. Pullout loads increase sharply above 1 in. — For 10-in. slabs at 7 days: 0 in. misalignment = 750 lb; 1 in. horizontal = 2,000 lb; 2 in. horizontal = 4,000 lb; 4 in. horizontal = 5,500 lb. Vertical misalignment produces higher loads than horizontal (tayabji-trr-1062, Table 8).

4. Analytical modeling infeasible — Three-dimensional modeling of dowel-concrete slippage and misalignment geometry too complex for available FE programs (SAP4, BMINES). Laboratory testing was necessary (tayabji-trr-1062, pp. 50-51).

5. Field noncompliance widespread but no distress observed — Georgia DOT study: 60-83% of implanted dowels out of specification tolerance, yet no dowel-related distress found at any evaluated joint. All joints started "working" within days of construction (tayabji-trr-1062, pp. 49-50, Tables 4-5).

6. FHWA does not specify numeric limits — Technical Advisory TS140.18 (1980) removed specific tolerance limits but cautioned that "close tolerances for dowel placement are extremely important for proper functioning of the slab and for long-term performance" (tayabji-trr-1062, p. 47).

7. Locked joints cause multiple distress types — Excessive restraint from misaligned dowels may produce transverse cracking, corner breaks, and spalling at concrete face around dowel. Once spalling occurs, load transfer effectiveness decreases (tayabji-trr-1062, p. 47).

8. No revision to current tolerances recommended — Due to limited laboratory data and insufficient field performance data, existing state agency tolerances (typically 1/4 in. per 18 in.) should remain, though data suggest greater tolerances may be acceptable (tayabji-trr-1062, p. 54).

Longitudinal Cracking of Jointed Plain Concrete Pavements in Louisiana: Field Investigation and Numerical Simulation

Journal International Journal of Pavement Research and Technology, Vol. 11, pp. 417–426 Sponsor Louisiana DOTD, FHWA (LTRC Project 124P)

Overview

Comprehensive field and numerical study demonstrating that longitudinal cracking is more prominent than transverse cracking in Louisiana JPCP — a distress type NOT currently predicted by AASHTO Pavement ME. Establishes that widened slabs and tied concrete shoulders, while reducing transverse cracking, significantly increase longitudinal cracking potential. Develops first empirical prediction model for longitudinal cracking.

Key Claims

1. Longitudinal cracking exceeds transverse cracking — Survey of 67.5 miles (43 projects, avg age 20.3 years) showed longitudinal cracking consistently higher than transverse cracking regardless of pavement age. IRI dramatically increased for roads >30 years (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 418-419, Fig. 2).

2. Widened slabs increase longitudinal cracking — Widening from 13 ft to 15 ft:

  • Transverse cracking: reduced from 40.9 to 6.7 ft/mi (84% reduction)
  • Longitudinal cracking: increased from 174 to 520 ft/mi (199% increase)
  • Critical fatigue damage location shifts from longitudinal edge to transverse joint (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 421-422, Fig. 5).

3. Tied concrete shoulders increase longitudinal cracking — Compared to curb sections:

  • Transverse cracking: reduced from 40.9 to 6.8 ft/mi
  • Longitudinal cracking: increased from 174 to 825 ft/mi (374% increase)
  • Concrete shoulders change length/width ratio, creating critical tensile stress at right wheel path (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 421-422).

4. Slab geometry controls cracking orientation — Longitudinal Cracking Ratio (LCR) = σCx/σCy (transverse/longitudinal critical tensile stress). LCR > 1.0 indicates definite longitudinal cracking. 127 of 288 FE cases showed longitudinal cracking potential (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 422-423).

5. FE simulation findings (ABAQUS, 288 cases):

  • Widened slab (12→15 ft): longitudinal cracking cases increased from 25 to 47
  • Tied concrete shoulder: 55 cases with longitudinal cracking likelihood
  • Tandem/tridem axles at transverse joints increase longitudinal cracking potential
  • Thinner slabs (8 in.) develop more longitudinal cracking than thicker (12 in.)
  • Less temperature gradient (warm regions) causes more longitudinal cracking (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 423-424, Fig. 6).

6. Construction problems confirmed — Inadequate longitudinal joint forming (plastic inserter method replaced by saw-cut/seal in 2000s) and inadequate base support identified as causes for premature localized cracking. One project: 86% slabs with longitudinal cracks, 4,163 ft/mi (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 420-421).

7. Empirical prediction model developed: `` log10(LC) = b0 + b1·TI + b2·L + b3·W + b4·(LW/D²) + b5·Shoulder + b6·Mr + b7·h·∛EBS ``

  • R² = 0.5423 (n = 35); LC = longitudinal cracking (ft/mi)
  • TI = Traffic Index (AADTT × directional × lane distribution × age)
  • Positive coefficients: W (width), Shoulder (tied concrete), TI (traffic)
  • Negative coefficients: L (length), Mr (subgrade modulus), h·∛EBS (base stiffness) (xiao-wu-2018-louisiana-longitudinal-cracking, pp. 424-425).

Notable Quotes

  • "Currently longitudinal cracking is not considered in pavement design. However, experience in Louisiana found that longitudinal cracking was more prominent than transverse cracking for jointed concrete pavements." (p. 425)
  • "Practically widened slab and tied shoulder have been proved to effectively reduce transverse and corner cracking, but the negative effect on longitudinal cracking potential needs to be carefully investigated and addressed." (p. 425)
  • "This study shows the necessity and possibility of developing a longitudinal cracking model for jointed concrete pavements." (p. 424)

Analysis of Cracking in Jointed Plain Concrete Pavements

Report FHWA-HRT-16-073, Federal Highway Administration Researchers University of Cambridge, Department of Engineering

Overview

Analyzes LTPP SPS-2 data to identify trends in longitudinal and transverse cracking. Proposes alternative mechanism for premature cracking: voiding in foundation soil due to localized plastic deformation from depressurization caused by slab curl, particularly problematic in dry regions with low cohesive strength soils.

Key Claims

1. Soil voiding mechanism — Premature cracking explained by voiding in foundation soil due to localized plastic deformation. Depressurization of soil caused by slab curl creates voids, particularly problematic for soils with low cohesive strength in dry regions (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis, pp. 2093-2100).

2. Two longitudinal crack patterns — (1) Single long crack ~1m from pavement edge adjacent to shoulder; (2) Short crack near centerline of slab. Most longitudinal cracks initiate from slab transverse edges (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis, pp. 2074-2084).

3. Transverse cracks initiate from shoulder edge — Most transverse cracks initiate from slab longitudinal edge close to the shoulder (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis, pp. 2082-2084).

4. Dry zones worse — Cracks occur earlier and are more severe in dry climatic zones compared to wet zones (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis, pp. 2069-2071).

5. Slab thickness and base type most sensitive — Severity of longitudinal and transverse cracking sensitive to slab thickness and base type. Slab width and strength have less clear effect (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis, pp. 2062-2066).

6. Edge pumping follows cracking — In Arkansas (wet zone), edge pumping occurred as result of cracking rather than vice versa (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis, pp. 2088-2090).

7. Slab curl + traffic + weak soil interaction — Premature cracking mechanism involves interaction of slab curl, vehicle loading, and foundation soils with low cohesive strengths (xu-cebon-2017-lttp-cracking-analysis, pp. 2054-2058).

Notable Quotes

  • "A plausible explanation for premature cracking in plain jointed concrete pavements is the occurrence of voiding in the foundation soil due to localized plastic deformation. This can occur because of depressurization of the soil caused by slab curl." (p. 2093-2100)

Investigation of Longitudinal Cracking in Widened Concrete Pavements

Journal The Baltic Journal of Road and Bridge Engineering 15(1), pp. 211-231 Sponsor Iowa Department of Transportation (TR-700), Iowa State University

Overview

Field investigation and FEA of longitudinal cracking in widened JPCP using Terrestrial Laser Scanner for curling/warping measurements and concrete coring. Identifies combination of excessive traffic, high curling/warping, inadequate support, pavement ageing, and skewed joints as primary causes.

Key Claims

1. Top-down cracks 0.6-1.2m from edge — Top-down longitudinal cracks observed in widened JPCP, mainly located within traffic lanes 0.60-1.20m (2-4ft) from slab edges, parallel to traffic direction (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1450-1453).

2. Five causes of longitudinal cracking — Combination of: (1) excessive loads, (2) high degrees of curling/warping, (3) inadequate subgrade support, (4) pavement ageing, (5) skewed joints (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1475-1477).

3. Traffic volume correlates with cracking — Sites subjected to higher truck traffic volume exhibited more significant number of longitudinal cracks (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1456-1457).

4. Curling/warping measurements confirm — Sites with higher degrees of curling and warping measurements exhibited significantly more longitudinal cracks than sites with lower degrees (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1458-1460).

5. Tied PCC shoulders outperform — Sites with tied PCC shoulders generally outperformed those with HMA and granular shoulders, even though tied PCC sites experienced relatively higher traffic volume (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1461-1464).

6. Late-afternoon paving reduces cracking — Site constructed in late afternoon, where concrete set during night-time and led to less built-in curling, only slightly exhibited longitudinal cracks (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1465-1467).

7. Skewed joints prone to cracking — FEA results indicate pavements with skewed joints are prone to longitudinal cracks compared to rectangular joints under all loading scenarios (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1468-1471).

8. Recommendations — At most 4.30m (14ft) for widened slab width; late-afternoon paving to avoid excessive upward built-in curling; avoid skewed joints and granular shoulders for widened JPCP with high truck traffic (yang-et-al-2020-widened-concrete-pavements, pp. 1477-1482).
